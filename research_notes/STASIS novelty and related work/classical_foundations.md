## Q1. Denning's working-set model (1968) and "Working Sets Past and Present" (1980): what does the working-set load control policy specify, and what did Denning claim about its optimality?

### Takeaway
The working-set policy is a *variable-space* memory policy: a process's resident set is held equal to W(t,θ), the set of distinct pages referenced in the last θ units of virtual time, and load control follows as a corollary — a process is only allowed to run if its whole working set is resident, so the multiprogramming level is whatever number of working sets fits in memory. Denning's optimality claim is narrow and specific: the working-set policy is *not* claimed optimal in absolute terms; rather VMIN (an optimal variable-space policy requiring θ-lookahead) faults at exactly the same instants as working-set for the same θ, and Denning's 1980 thesis is that no *cheaper non-lookahead* policy than working sets is likely to be found.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Peter J. Denning, "The Working Set Model for Program Behavior," *Proc. ACM Symposium on Operating System Principles (SOSP)* 1967, and *Communications of the ACM* 11(5), May 1968, pp. 323–333. Scanned copy mirrored as a course reading — [Waterloo course readings mirror](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~brecht/courses/epfl/Possible-Readings/vm-and-gc/working-set-model-denning-sosp-1967.pdf)
- Citation: Peter J. Denning, "Thrashing: Its Causes and Prevention," *Proc. AFIPS Fall Joint Computer Conference (FJCC)* 1968, vol. 33, pp. 915–922. Bibliographic details confirmed via a Springer reference list — [Springer, Acta Informatica reference list](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771); scanned full text at [Waterloo mirror](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~brecht/courses/epfl/Possible-Readings/vm-and-gc/thrashing-denning-afips-1968.pdf)
- Denning 1968 defines thrashing as "excessive overhead and severe performance degradation or collapse caused by too much paging" — [Waterloo mirror of Denning AFIPS 1968](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~brecht/courses/epfl/Possible-Readings/vm-and-gc/thrashing-denning-afips-1968.pdf) (quoted via search snippet of the paper's introduction)
- Denning 1968 argues the cause is the *interaction* of program behavior, the paging algorithm and hardware capacity — explicitly **not** that programs are inherently unsuited to paging: "troubles with paged memory systems arise not from any misconception about program behavior" — [Waterloo mirror of Denning AFIPS 1968](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~brecht/courses/epfl/Possible-Readings/vm-and-gc/thrashing-denning-afips-1968.pdf)
- Citation: Peter J. Denning, "Working Sets Past and Present," *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering* SE-6(1), January 1980, pp. 64–84. An earlier and longer (88 pp.) version exists as Purdue CS Technical Report 78-276 (1978) — [Denning publication list, ae-info.org](https://www.ae-info.org/ae/User/Denning_Peter/Publications); [Purdue CS Tech Reports](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cstech/208)
- Definition per a Stanford quals summary of Denning 1980: the working set is the collection of recently referenced segments; "at time t, the working set size is the number of distinct segments referenced in the window from t−θ+1 to t"; "the working set policy keeps a program's resident set equal to its working set at all times" — [Stanford quals notes on Denning 1980](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www-db.stanford.edu/~daswani/quals/Denning80.htm) (secondary)
- Role of θ: "θ trades paging load against resident set size. A larger θ gives a larger mean resident set and longer mean interfault times." Fixed-space policies read θ as the resident-set size; variable-space policies read θ as a bound on segment residence time — [Stanford quals notes on Denning 1980](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www-db.stanford.edu/~daswani/quals/Denning80.htm) (secondary)
- Optimality: VMIN is the optimal variable-space memory policy, uses lookahead of θ, and "generates segment faults exactly when the working set policy does for the same θ" — [Stanford quals notes on Denning 1980](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www-db.stanford.edu/~daswani/quals/Denning80.htm) (secondary)
- Stated thesis of the 1980 paper: it "outlines [the] argument that it will be unlikely to find a cheaper nonlookahead memory policy than working sets" — [Stanford quals notes on Denning 1980](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www-db.stanford.edu/~daswani/quals/Denning80.htm); corroborated by a second independent quals summary — [Stanford quals summary, Working_Sets-Denning-1980.txt](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www.stanford.edu/~emrek/quals/summaries/Working_Sets-Denning-1980.txt)
- Denning 1980 also generalizes to the Generalized Working Set (GWS): a segment is retained if its accumulated retention cost since its prior reference is at most θ — [Stanford quals notes on Denning 1980](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www-db.stanford.edu/~daswani/quals/Denning80.htm) (secondary)
- Denning 1980 is explicit that working sets are "tools for measuring memory demand, but not models of program behavior" — [Stanford quals notes on Denning 1980](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www-db.stanford.edu/~daswani/quals/Denning80.htm) (secondary)
- The load-control corollary as restated by Denning: if memory is overcommitted, at least one process will not have its working set fully resident and will therefore run inefficiently; the remedy is to keep only *whole* working sets of active processes in memory. Course statement of the scheduling rule: "process not executed unless WS in memory" — [Stanford CS140 lecture notes](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/cs/cs140/cs140.1088/lectures/sum0708/lecture13.pdf) (secondary)
- The set of processes whose working sets are resident is the **balance set**; inactive processes' working sets migrate to disk; "frequent changes to that group cause overhead, because the working sets keep shifting" — [UW–Madison CS736 lecture notes on working sets](https://swift.sites.cs.wisc.edu/classes/cs736-sp07/lectures/working-set.html) (secondary)
- Denning returned to the topic in 2021: Peter J. Denning, "Working Set Analytics," covering the history of working-set theory and its resistance to thrashing — [working-set-analytics-2021.pdf (EPFL mirror)](https://moodle.epfl.ch/pluginfile.php/3296507/mod_resource/content/1/working-set-analytics-2021.pdf). (Published as *ACM Computing Surveys* 53(6), 2021 — note the CSUR citation is from training knowledge and should be re-verified before citing.)

### Inferences
- The working-set policy's load control is *implicit and emergent*, not an explicit scheduler: the policy specifies a per-process memory rule (resident set = W(t,θ)), and the multiprogramming level is then a derived quantity (as many working sets as fit). This matters for a novelty argument: the classical model never specifies *which* process to exclude when memory is short — exclusion is whatever falls out of the memory accounting.
- Denning's optimality claim is about *fault-count equivalence to a lookahead-optimal policy at a given θ*, not about system throughput or fairness. Any thesis claiming to beat working-set load control on throughput or fairness is not contradicting Denning's theorem, because that theorem is silent on both.

### Gaps
- The exact wording of Denning 1968's load-control prescription could not be verified from the primary text (scanned bitmap; no OCR available locally). The formulation above is from secondary summaries and should be checked against pp. 915–922 before citing verbatim.
- The 1980 paper's own comparison table of load-control policies (if any) was not retrievable; IEEE Xplore is paywalled and no author copy was found.

## Q2. Chu & Opderbeck's page-fault-frequency (PFF) algorithm: definition, criterion, measured results vs working set

### Takeaway
PFF is a variable-space replacement algorithm that acts *only at page-fault times*: it measures the interval since the previous fault and compares it to a fixed threshold T. If the interval exceeded T, every page not referenced during that interval is released (the resident set shrinks, possibly a lot); otherwise nothing is evicted and the allocation grows by one frame. It is an inter-fault-time-triggered approximation to the working set that is far cheaper to implement because it samples reference bits only at faults rather than continuously. **I could not retrieve the actual measured PFF-vs-working-set numbers** from any of the three Chu/Opderbeck papers.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Wesley W. Chu and Holger Opderbeck, "The Page Fault Frequency Replacement Algorithm," *Proc. AFIPS Fall Joint Computer Conference* 1972, vol. 41, pp. 597–609. (Year 1972 appears in search results but was **not** independently verified; see Gaps.) — [search result noting a 1972 date for the Chu & Opderbeck work](https://www.academia.edu/58449733/Program_behavior_and_the_page_fault_frequency_replacement_algorithm)
- Citation: Holger Opderbeck and Wesley W. Chu, "Performance of the Page Fault Frequency Replacement Algorithm in a Multiprogramming Environment," *IFIP Congress* 1974, pp. 235–241 — [DBLP: Holger Opderbeck](https://vldb.org/dblp/db/indices/a-tree/o/Opderbeck:Holger.html)
- Citation: Wesley W. Chu and Holger Opderbeck, "Analysis of the PFF Replacement Algorithm via a Semi-Markov Model," *Communications of the ACM* 19(5), 1976, pp. 298–304 — [DBLP: Holger Opderbeck](https://vldb.org/dblp/db/indices/a-tree/o/Opderbeck:Holger.html)
- Citation: Wesley W. Chu and Holger Opderbeck, "Program Behavior and the Page-Fault-Frequency Replacement Algorithm," *Computer* (IEEE), 1976 — [academia.edu record](https://www.academia.edu/58449733/Program_behavior_and_the_page_fault_frequency_replacement_algorithm)
- Citation: Holger Opderbeck, "The Page Fault Frequency Replacement Algorithm in a Multi-Programming Environment," Ph.D. dissertation, UCLA, 1973, advisor Wesley W. Chu — [Mathematics Genealogy Project](https://www.mathgenealogy.org/id.php?id=116843)
- Algorithm definition, verbatim from the paper's own description as indexed: "The PFF algorithm measures the inter page fault intervals during execution. At page fault times, it compares them with an a priori selected threshold T." If the interval is longer than T, pages not referenced during that interval are released; "otherwise no page is dumped and the allocation increases by one page frame" — [academia.edu record of Chu & Opderbeck, "Program Behavior and the Page Fault Frequency Replacement Algorithm"](https://www.academia.edu/58449733/Program_behavior_and_the_page_fault_frequency_replacement_algorithm)
- Experimental setting: reference patterns were measured on the UCLA Sigma-7 system using a Sigma-7 interpreter to obtain stack-distance probabilities for various program types; the paper "discusses the performance of the PFF algorithm in both uniprogramming and multiprogramming environments" — [academia.edu record](https://www.academia.edu/58449733/Program_behavior_and_the_page_fault_frequency_replacement_algorithm)
- PFF controls *how many* frames a process gets, not *which* pages — it is an approximation to the working set driven by the observed fault rate (faults per reference): raise the allocation when the fault rate is too high, take frames away when it is too low — [U. Washington CSE451 lecture, "Page Fault Frequency"](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse451/99wi/Lectures/8-vm/tsld017.htm) (secondary)
- Documented weakness of PFF: it "does not perform well during transition periods (shift to a new locality)," and it is hard to distinguish a change of locality from a change in working-set size — [NYU Gottlieb OS lecture notes](https://cs.nyu.edu/~gottlieb/courses/2000s/2001-02-spring/os/lectures/lecture-20.html) (secondary)

### Inferences
- PFF and the working-set policy differ in *when* they act, not in what they are trying to achieve: WS maintains the resident set continuously in virtual time, PFF updates it only at fault instants. The practical consequence — and the reason PFF was attractive in the 1970s — is that PFF needs no virtual-time clock or periodic reference-bit sampling.
- PFF's "release everything not referenced since the last fault" branch is a *cliff*: after one long inter-fault interval a process can lose most of its resident set at once. This is a plausible structural explanation for the transition-period weakness the course literature reports, but I found no primary source that frames it that way.

### Gaps
- **No measured PFF-vs-working-set numbers were obtained.** The 1972 AFIPS, 1974 IFIP and 1976 CACM papers are paywalled or scanned-image-only; the one freely reachable scan (an aminer-hosted SOSP-collection PDF) is a CCITT bitmap with no text layer. Any claim of the form "PFF achieved X% fewer faults than WS at equal mean resident set" must be sourced from the printed papers.
- The exact venue/year of the *original* PFF paper is unresolved in the sources I could reach: one record labels the *Computer* article 2000 while giving a 1976 DOI, and another gives 1972 for "the Chu and Opderbeck work." Treat 1972 (AFIPS FJCC) as the likely original and 1976 as the *Computer* retrospective, but verify both against ACM DL / IEEE Xplore.
- Whether Chu & Opderbeck's threshold T is in virtual time, real time, or references was not confirmed from a primary source.

## Q3. The L=S criterion, the 50% criterion, and Denning's comparison of load-control policies

### Takeaway
The canonical comparison is Denning, Kahn, Leroudier, Potier and Suri, "Optimal Multiprogramming" (*Acta Informatica*, 1976), which evaluates three load-control heuristics — the **knee criterion** (keep each resident set at the knee of its lifetime curve), **L=S** (keep mean inter-fault lifetime L at least the page-swap time S), and the **50% criterion** (keep the paging device ~50% busy). Its conclusion is a robustness ordering, not an optimality proof: the knee criterion is the most robust but hardest to implement; the 50% criterion is the easiest to implement and the *least* robust. The authors are explicit that these are heuristics and cannot be proved optimal.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: P. J. Denning, K. C. Kahn, J. Leroudier, D. Potier, R. Suri, "Optimal Multiprogramming," *Acta Informatica* 7(2), 1976, pp. 197–216 (DOI 10.1007/BF00265771) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771); [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FBF00265771)
- Abstract framing: "Three heuristics for controlling the multiprogramming load to maximize system work capacity are studied. Each allows the highest load possible subject to a given constraint." The three are the knee, L=S and 50% criteria — [Springer, Acta Informatica 10.1007/BF00265771](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771)
- Knee criterion: keep resident sets near the knees of their lifetime curves — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771)
- L=S: keep the inter-fault lifetime at least as large as the page-swap time — i.e. "keep the system life time to approximately that of the page swap time" — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771)
- 50% criterion: "The 50% criterion constrains the load so that the paging device is busy approximately half the time." — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771)
- Robustness result (queueing-network evaluation): "the knee criterion, which is the most difficult to implement, is the most robust, while the easily implemented 50% criterion is the least robust" — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771)
- Explicit disclaimer of optimality in the follow-on literature: "these criteria are not based on mathematical models and cannot be proved to be optimal" — [UBC Technical Report TR-80-05](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/tr/1980/TR-80-05.pdf)
- Earlier primary source for L=S: P. J. Denning and K. C. Kahn, "An L=S criterion for optimal multiprogramming," 1976 (ACM SIGMETRICS / Int. Symp. on Computer Performance Modeling, Measurement and Evaluation) — [Springer reference list](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771)
- Denning also published a broader treatment, "Control of Performance in Multiple-Resource Systems" — [World Scientific](https://worldscientific.com/doi/10.1142/9781848163966_0009)

### Inferences
- Note the ordering of the field's own verdict: the policy that is *easiest* to deploy (50%) is the one the authors trust least. A 2026 thesis can fairly characterize the classical load-control literature as having converged on *no* implementable, provably optimal criterion — only a robustness ranking among heuristics.
- All three criteria are *scalar aggregate* criteria over the whole system (lifetime, device utilization, knee position). None of them is a per-process criterion, which is why none of them has anything to say about *which* process bears the cost of a load reduction.

### Gaps
- The paper's full text (and therefore its exact quantitative robustness results — e.g. how much throughput each criterion loses under mis-tuning) is paywalled; Springer redirects to an IdP. The abstract-level claims above are all I could verify.

## Q4. Documented failure mode of thrashing control; fairness between processes under load control; was fair rotation of deactivated processes ever proposed or evaluated?

### Takeaway
Two failure modes are documented: (a) *instability/overhead from churn* — frequent changes to the balance set cost more than they save because working sets keep being shifted in and out; and (b) *over-aggressive suspension collapsing the multiprogramming level*, which production systems guard against with an explicit floor rather than with any principled criterion. On fairness: **the classical literature is effectively silent.** I found no classical paper that formulates fairness between deactivated processes as a goal, and no paper that proposes or evaluates *fair rotation* of suspended processes. The only fairness-shaped mechanisms I found are engineering safeguards in production OS documentation (AIX), not research results.

### Cited Findings
- Churn failure mode: "frequent changes to that group [the balance set] cause overhead, because the working sets keep shifting" — [UW–Madison CS736 lecture notes](https://swift.sites.cs.wisc.edu/classes/cs736-sp07/lectures/working-set.html) (secondary)
- The remedy as classically stated: "let only processes with large enough resident sets run, and suspend certain processes to accomplish this" — [SUNY Buffalo CSE421 lecture, "Load control"](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~bina/cse421/spring00/LEC78MemMgt/tsld029.htm) (secondary)
- Survey-level statement that load control = process suspension/swapping after thrashing is detected, with 4.4BSD, AIX on the RS/6000, and HP-UX 10.0 on HP 9000 given as the representative implementations — [Jiang & Zhang, related work, USENIX Annual Linux Showcase 2001](https://static.usenix.org/publications/library/proceedings/als01/full_papers/jiang/jiang_html/node9.html)
- HP-UX's extreme form: a `serialize()` command that, once thrashing is detected, runs processes **one at a time** — [Jiang & Zhang, USENIX ALS 2001](https://static.usenix.org/publications/library/proceedings/als01/full_papers/jiang/jiang_html/node9.html)
- AIX load control, the most concretely documented production policy: process 0 (the scheduler) checks once per second whether memory is overcommitted; the system-wide threshold is `v_repage_hi`; each process is judged against `v_repage_proc` to decide whether it is a suspension candidate; every process passing the `-p` and `-e` tests is marked for suspension — [IBM AIX docs, "Memory load control algorithm"](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_71/performance/mem_load_control_alg.html)
- AIX marks a process but suspends it only when it next reaches the CPU in user mode, so system work on its behalf is not interrupted; newly created processes are also suspended during the interval, which keeps new work from entering — [IBM AIX docs](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_71/performance/mem_load_control_alg.html)
- AIX floor on multiprogramming level: `v_min_process` (the `-m` value) sets the minimum number of active processes; suspension stops before the count would drop below it — [IBM AIX docs, "The v_min_process parameter"](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_73/performance/v_min_proc_param.html)
- **The closest thing to fair rotation found anywhere:** AIX reactivation. After a "safe interval" with no thrashing (length `v_sec_wait`), "suspended processes are reactivated by priority, then by the order in which they were suspended." Each second, the number reactivated is one-fifth of the active processes or a rising lower bound, whichever is greater — roughly a 20%-per-second increase in multiprogramming level. If overcommitment recurs, reactivation halts and the marked processes are suspended again — [IBM AIX docs, "Memory load control algorithm"](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_71/performance/mem_load_control_alg.html)
- AIX anti-starvation guarantee: `v_exempt_secs` sets the minimum number of seconds of activity a process receives after reactivation before it can be suspended again — [IBM AIX docs](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_71/performance/mem_load_control_alg.html)
- AIX's suspension rule also means suspended processes "are not reactivated until a subsequent interval passes during which no potential thrashing condition exists" — [IBM AIX docs](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_71/performance/mem_load_control_alg.html)
- Research-level alternative explicitly positioned *against* load control: Jiang & Zhang state their approach "pursues the same objective as load control but acts on page replacement algorithms rather than whole processes," allowing a careful CPU-vs-memory utilization tradeoff — [Jiang & Zhang, USENIX ALS 2001](https://static.usenix.org/publications/library/proceedings/als01/full_papers/jiang/jiang_html/node9.html)
- Database-side analogue of load control: the "Half-and-Half" algorithm monitors DBMS state to dynamically control the multiprogramming level — [U. Wisconsin CS Technical Report #880](https://research.cs.wisc.edu/techreports/viewreport.php?report=880)
- Transaction-processing analogue: "Adaptive Load Control in Transaction Processing Systems" — [academia.edu record](https://www.academia.edu/70434605/Adaptive_Load_Control_in_Transaction_Processing_Systems)

### Inferences
- **The silence is real and is the strongest novelty hook here.** Across the classical load-control literature I could reach (Denning 1968, Denning 1980 summaries, Denning–Kahn L=S, Denning et al. 1976 three-criteria comparison) the decision variable is always *the multiprogramming level* — a scalar. The question "given that we must deactivate k processes, which k, and for how long each?" is not posed as a research question. Victim choice is treated as an implementation detail delegated to whatever memory or fault-rate accounting is already running.
- Consequently, *fair rotation of deactivated processes was, as far as these sources show, never proposed or evaluated as a research contribution.* The one rotation-like mechanism (AIX priority-then-FIFO reactivation plus `v_exempt_secs` exemption) appears in **vendor documentation**, with no accompanying evaluation, no fairness metric, and no comparison against alternatives. It is engineering folklore, not a studied policy. A thesis claiming novelty on fair rotation should cite AIX as prior *practice* while noting the absence of any published evaluation.
- Likewise, the AIX `v_min_process` floor and HP-UX `serialize()` bracket the design space at its two extremes (never go below N active; in the worst case go all the way to 1) without anything principled in between — again, no published evaluation of the fairness consequences.
- A second genuine gap: the classical criteria are all *throughput*-oriented. I found no classical work that reports per-process latency, waiting-time distribution, or tail behaviour under load control. Thrashing-control papers report system throughput and page-fault rates; they do not report whether some process waited 100× longer than another.

### Gaps
- I found **no** classical paper (1968–1990) that studies fairness, starvation, or rotation among suspended/deactivated processes under thrashing load control. A targeted search for "fairness," "starvation," "deactivated processes," and "multiprogramming level" returned only the AIX documentation and unrelated DB load-control work. This is a negative result worth stating plainly in the thesis, but note the limitation: it rests on web-reachable sources, and a definitive negative claim should be backed by a search of ACM DL and IEEE Xplore full-text indices (which I could not query) before being asserted as "never."
- No source was found that documents a *measured* failure of thrashing control — e.g. a paper reporting that load control itself induced starvation or oscillation in a real system. The "churn" failure mode is stated in course notes only.
- AIX parameter *default values* are not given on the algorithm page; they are on the `schedo` tuning page, which I did not fetch.

## Q5. Historical OS implementations of swapping/deactivation as thrashing control: how was the victim chosen, and did any rotate fairly?

### Takeaway
Multics motivated the working-set model; classic UNIX, 4.xBSD, VMS, AIX, HP-UX and Windows NT all implement some form of deactivation, but **victim selection is heterogeneous and mostly ad hoc**: residence time plus priority (classic UNIX), guaranteed-minimum overrun then inactivity (VMS SWAPPER), repage rate (AIX), lowest page-fault rate (Windows working-set trimming). Only swap-*in* in classic UNIX/BSD is clearly longest-waiting-first, i.e. FIFO-like and therefore incidentally fair; no system I found rotates *outswap* victims fairly.

### Cited Findings
- **Multics** is the origin story: Denning proposed the working-set idea in the 1960s when Multics exhibited thrashing, and Denning 1968 names Multics among systems whose performance thrashing could seriously degrade — [UW–Madison CS736 lecture notes](https://swift.sites.cs.wisc.edu/classes/cs736-sp07/lectures/working-set.html) (secondary)
- **Classic UNIX swapper:** the swapper picks an outswap victim from processes resident for at least two seconds, weighting blocked and ready processes by priority plus residence time; swapping can also be triggered because some process has been swapped out on disk for too long — [UMBC OS lecture notes (1996)](https://userpages.cs.umbc.edu/motteler/teaching/os/os96a/notes/n12.txt) (secondary; describes older UNIX generally, not 4.3BSD specifically)
- **Swap-in is longest-waiting-first:** 2.11BSD's `vm_sched.c` loop selects, among ready swapped-out processes, the one that has been out longest, using its residence-time field adjusted by nice value; its comments note the scheduler sleeps when it cannot free enough core — [2.11BSD vm_sched.c source](https://www.retro11.de/ouxr/211bsd/usr/src/sys/sys/vm_sched.c.html) (primary source code)
- Historical note: original UNIX swapped *whole processes*; the page-level clock was introduced to replace that approach — Babaoglu and Joy, "Converting a Swap-Based System to Do Paging in an Architecture Lacking Page-Reference Bits" (SOSP 1981) — as described in [Chalmers 4BSD virtual memory slides](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/edu/year/2012/course/EDA092/SLIDES/8-bsd_virtual_memory.pdf) (secondary)
- **4BSD replacement** is a *global* clock (second-chance) policy run by the pageout daemon, not the swapper; the two-handed variant uses each page's reference bit with two tunable parameters, scan rate and handspread — [Chalmers 4BSD VM slides](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/edu/year/2012/course/EDA092/SLIDES/8-bsd_virtual_memory.pdf); [UCR CS202 reading](https://intra.engr.ucr.edu/~htseng/classes/cs202_2021wi/Preview/UnixVMWSClockPreview.pdf) (secondary)
- **VMS:** the paging algorithm adjusts each process's *working-set limit*, while the SWAPPER process performs the larger memory moves — [OpenVMS Operating System Concepts, 2nd ed., §4.4 "OpenVMS swapping algorithm"](https://www.globalspec.com/reference/24130/203279/section-4-4-openvms-swapping-algorithm) (the full text returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch; content via search index)
- **VMS SWAPPER victim order** (per a patent describing VMS memory allocation): SWAPPER first trims the modified page list, then trims any working set grown past its *guaranteed minimum*, and then swaps or trims inactive and then active processes — [US Patent 5,517,643, "Method of allocating memory among a plurality of processes of a computer system"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5517643)
- VMS is classified in the research literature as the representative *local replacement* system: it partitions memory into independent areas per process group, and this scheme "can be difficult to administer" — [Jiang & Zhang, USENIX ALS 2001](https://static.usenix.org/publications/library/proceedings/als01/full_papers/jiang/jiang_html/node9.html)
- **Windows NT balance set manager:** "the closest thing Windows NT has to a swapper"; the *balance set* is the sum of all inswapped working sets; it is a system thread that "wakes up every second" — [HPI Operating Systems Architecture, Windows memory management internals](https://osm.hpi.de/bsArch/2005/Unit05/5.4_WinMemManagInternals_2.pdf) (secondary, NT/Win2000 era)
- Windows trimming victim selection: trimming runs "from current down to minimal WS for processes with lowest page fault rate" — i.e. **low-fault processes are trimmed first** — [HPI, Windows physical memory management](https://osm.hpi.de/bsArch/2006/Slides/Unit05/5.4_WinPhysMemManagement.pdf) (secondary)
- Windows outswap precondition: a process is outswapped only if it has no nonpageable kernel stacks; a separate thread then shrinks its working-set limit toward zero; the balance set manager can also mark kernel stack pages pageable for threads in long user-mode waits — [HPI, Windows memory management internals](https://osm.hpi.de/bsArch/2005/Unit05/5.4_WinMemManagInternals_2.pdf) (secondary)
- The working set manager is a routine that runs in the context of the balance set manager system thread — [HPI, Windows memory management](https://osm.hpi.de/bsArch/2005/Unit05/5.2_WinMemManag_2.pdf) (secondary)
- **AIX** victim selection is by *repage rate*: `v_repage_proc` per process against `v_repage_hi` system-wide (see Q4) — [IBM AIX docs](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_71/performance/mem_load_control_alg.html)
- **System V anecdote / counter-example:** a Usenet post reports that System V sometimes chose *the process requesting memory* as the outswap candidate, which the poster considered a poor choice — [comp.sys.att archive, July 1990](https://ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/mirrors/www.tuhs.org/Unix_Usenet/comp.sys.att/1990-July/002058.html) (low-reliability source: an unrefereed Usenet post; cite only as anecdote)

### Inferences
- Every documented victim rule is a *greedy* rule keyed on a resource statistic (residence time, working-set overrun, repage rate, fault rate) — never on how much deactivation a process has already suffered. The only history-aware mechanisms anywhere are AIX's `v_exempt_secs` exemption and classic UNIX/BSD swap-*in* by longest-time-out. So: no system rotates outswap victims; two systems give a weak anti-starvation guarantee on the re-entry side.
- Windows' "trim processes with the lowest page-fault rate first" is notable because it is the *opposite* of AIX's repage-rate rule. The literature therefore does not even agree on the sign of the correct victim heuristic — a useful observation for a thesis arguing that victim selection has never been treated as a first-class design problem.

### Gaps
- The exact 4.3BSD outswap victim rule was not verified from a primary source. The authoritative places to check are 4.3BSD `sys/vm_sched.c` / `kern_synch.c` and McKusick, Bostic, Karels & Quarterman, *The Design and Implementation of the 4.3BSD UNIX Operating System*. The 2.11BSD source above covers swap-in only.
- No Multics primary source (Multics System Programmers' Manual, or Organick's *The Multics System*) was retrieved; the Multics load-control mechanism is asserted here only via secondary course notes.
- OpenVMS §4.6 "Thrashing" returned HTTP 403 and could not be read; its content is referenced only via search indexing.
- No current Microsoft documentation or modern (Windows 10/11) victim-selection logic was found; *Windows Internals* (latest edition) is the needed primary source. All Windows claims above are NT/Windows 2000 era.
- Tanenbaum (*Modern Operating Systems*) and Silberschatz (*Operating System Concepts*) textbook treatments of the balance set / swapper were **not** directly consulted — the textbooks are not freely fetchable. The thesis should cite them directly for the textbook-level account.

## Q6. Critiques of working-set load control: overhead, estimation difficulty, and why modern systems abandoned it

### Takeaway
Three distinct critiques are documented: (1) **implementation cost** — exact working-set maintenance requires continuous virtual-time reference sampling and is "impractical to implement in a real system," so every deployed system uses an approximation (PFF, WSCLOCK, periodic trimming); (2) **estimation difficulty** — θ must be tuned, and transitions between localities are hard to distinguish from genuine growth; (3) **model mismatch with modern systems** — the per-process, one-address-space-per-execution-context assumption no longer holds (threads share address spaces; the page cache belongs to no process), and the working set does not itself answer the question a replacement policy must answer. I found **no vendor statement or definitive historical record** documenting a decision to abandon working-set load control; the "abandonment" narrative should be presented as inference, not fact.

### Cited Findings
- Citation for the main engineering critique and the standard fix: Richard Carr and John L. Hennessy, "WSCLOCK — A Simple and Effective Algorithm for Virtual Memory Management," *Proc. 8th ACM Symposium on Operating Systems Principles (SOSP)* 1981, pp. 87–95 — [NYU CS3224 readings copy (p87-carr.pdf)](https://cse.engineering.nyu.edu/jsterling/cs3224/Readings/p87-carr.pdf) (direct fetch failed with "certificate rejected"; citation from search indexing)
- Carr & Hennessy's framing: thrashing occurs when so many tasks are active that the sum of their memory needs exceeds main memory, and WSCLOCK is presented as a way to **keep working-set load control while cutting its overhead** — [search index of p87-carr.pdf](https://cse.engineering.nyu.edu/jsterling/cs3224/Readings/p87-carr.pdf)
- Impracticality of the exact policy: "the WS algorithm, as strictly defined, [is] impractical to implement in a real system," and approximations are commonly used instead — [Stony Brook CSE306 Lecture 19, Virtual Memory 2](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~youngkwon/cse306/Lecture19_Virtual_Memory_2.pdf) (secondary)
- Overhead vs. utilization tension: "in order to be efficient, you need to know how much memory is needed in order to keep the CPU busy. If you don't have enough jobs on the queue, and just in general, implementing all of the overhead associated with working set is going to lead to the CPU being idle" — [Berkeley CS162 Sp09 student lecture notes](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs162/sp09/Students/Lecture_Notes_09.s/0316-ChrisFletcher.pdf) (low-reliability: student-transcribed lecture notes)
- Estimation difficulty: the working set "is hard to compute exactly"; with PFF "it is hard to tell between changes in locality and changes in size of working set"; PFF "does not perform well during transition periods" — [NYU Gottlieb OS lecture notes](https://cs.nyu.edu/~gottlieb/courses/2000s/2001-02-spring/os/lectures/lecture-20.html) (secondary)
- Modern-systems critique: "in modern systems there is no such one to one mapping anymore. There are multiple threads executing within the same address space"; and "there are memory consumers that are not readily identifiable with any execution context at all: file system cache"; and crucially "working set is not page replacement algorithms at all. That is, it doesn't answer to the question 'System is low on memory, what page should be reclaimed?'" — [cofault.com, "working set is not relevant"](https://cofault.com/working-set.html) (**single-author opinion piece, not peer-reviewed; use as a framing argument, not evidence**)
- Local replacement (the family working-set load control belongs to) is rejected by current practice: "researchers and practitioners largely agree a local policy is not an effective solution for virtual memory management"; Linux uses global replacement — [Jiang & Zhang, USENIX ALS 2001](https://static.usenix.org/publications/library/proceedings/als01/full_papers/jiang/jiang_html/node9.html)
- Equal and proportional allocation — the two common local-allocation schemes — "cannot capture each program's changing memory demand," so memory may be poorly used — [Jiang & Zhang, USENIX ALS 2001](https://static.usenix.org/publications/library/proceedings/als01/full_papers/jiang/jiang_html/node9.html)
- Working-set approximation in practice is what survived: Windows' periodic working-set trimming by the balance set manager (see Q5) is the working-set idea reduced to a once-per-second heuristic — [HPI Windows memory management internals](https://osm.hpi.de/bsArch/2005/Unit05/5.4_WinMemManagInternals_2.pdf)
- Denning himself revisited and defended the model as recently as 2021, in "Working Set Analytics," which discusses working-set theory's resistance to thrashing — [working-set-analytics-2021.pdf (EPFL mirror)](https://moodle.epfl.ch/pluginfile.php/3296507/mod_resource/content/1/working-set-analytics-2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- The honest historiography is: working-set *replacement* was replaced by cheap global approximations (clock, WSCLOCK, LRU-approximations); working-set *load control* was never replaced by anything principled — it was replaced by (a) a lot more RAM, (b) global replacement, and (c) crude OOM-killer-style last resorts. That asymmetry is the opening a 2026 thesis on thrashing control occupies.
- Carr & Hennessy (1981) is the pivotal citation: it concedes the working-set policy's overhead critique *while* accepting its load-control goal. Any thesis arguing "working-set load control was abandoned for overhead reasons" should cite WSCLOCK as the evidence that the field tried to keep it cheaply rather than discard it.

### Gaps
- Carr & Hennessy's full text could not be fetched ("certificate rejected" on the NYU host). Its specific overhead numbers, its WSCLOCK-vs-PFF comparison, and whether it discusses victim choice or fairness are all unverified. This is the single most important source to obtain from ACM DL.
- **I found no authoritative account — no vendor statement, no retrospective paper, no documented decision — of *why* working-set load control was dropped.** The explanation above (implementation overhead + thread/shared-address-space mismatch + rise of global clock approximations) is a synthesis across informal and dated sources, and should be presented as such.
- No dedicated *survey* of thrashing control was found. Denning 1980 and Denning 2021 are the nearest things, and both are authored by the model's originator, so neither is a neutral survey. If the thesis needs "there is no modern survey of thrashing control," that claim is consistent with what I could find, but it is a negative result from web-reachable sources only.
- Denning 2021 "Working Set Analytics" could not be fetched (the EPFL Moodle PDF returned HTTP 404 at fetch time, although it appears in the search index). Its content is therefore uncited here beyond its existence and topic.

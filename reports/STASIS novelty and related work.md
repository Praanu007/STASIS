# STASIS occupies a plumbed but unoccupied seam

**The defensible novelty in STASIS is narrow, structural, and real: the control signal lives in a pluggable BPF CPU scheduler while the actuator lives in the memory cgroup, and nothing in the surveyed literature or code closes that loop across the two subsystems.** Everything else — page-fault frequency as a thrashing signal (Denning 1968; Chu & Opderbeck, early 1970s), suspending the thrasher (Denning's load control, AIX, HP-UX `serialize()`), PSI-driven closed-loop actuation (Meta's Senpai/TMO, in production since 2021 saving [20–32% of memory per server](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/06/20/data-infrastructure/transparent-memory-offloading-more-memory-at-a-fraction-of-the-cost-and-power/)), freeze-on-thrash (tobixen's [thrash-protect](https://github.com/tobixen/thrash-protect)), and working-set admission control (Google [Autopilot](https://john.e-wilkes.com/papers/2020-EuroSys-Autopilot.pdf), xTier, TierBPF) — is occupied prior art with named owners. The most dangerous prior art is **not** Denning: it is thrash-protect, a working open-source daemon that already detects thrashing, already freezes the victim, already uses page-fault counts as one of its three victim-selection algorithms, and already does a crude rotation of frozen PIDs. The opportunity is that thrash-protect's README documents its own pathologies — "Thrash-protect is not optimized to be 'fair'", no PSI input, ineffective whitelisting, zram false positives — and **no published critique or reproduction study of it exists**, so measuring those pathologies against STASIS is new data rather than a re-derivation. A direct code search of the October 2026 upstream `sched-ext/scx` tree hardens the structural claim from an absence of evidence into a checkable fact: the per-CPU PSI and vmstat accessors (including `pgmajfault`) are already declared in the project's own `scx/percpu.bpf.h`, and **no scheduler in the tree calls them**. The practical recommendation is to spend the dissertation's novelty budget on three things in strict order — named-baseline comparison, formal control-theoretic analysis of the existing law, and real workloads — of which the first two leave the pre-registration entirely untouched.

## thrash-protect, not Denning, is the prior art that will decide the viva

The reflexive viva attack is "this is Denning re-implemented," and it is legitimate: detector (page-fault frequency), actuator (suspend a process), and admission rule (demand ≤ frames) are all Denning-era, and a textbook states STASIS's architecture in one sentence — under overload "the operating system selects a process to suspend" ([Working set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_set)). But Denning is the *easy* attack, because his optimality claim is narrow: VMIN "generates segment faults exactly when the working set policy does for the same θ" ([Denning 1980, via Stanford quals notes](https://carlstrom.com/stanford/quals/mirror/www-db.stanford.edu/~daswani/quals/Denning80.htm) — secondary source), which is a fault-count equivalence result that is **silent on throughput, latency and fairness**. The classical load-control literature is likewise silent on the question STASIS actually answers. In Denning, Kahn, Leroudier, Potier and Suri's canonical comparison of the knee, L=S and 50% criteria, the decision variable is always *the multiprogramming level* — a scalar — and the authors' own conclusion is a robustness ordering, not an optimality proof: "the knee criterion, which is the most difficult to implement, is the most robust, while the easily implemented 50% criterion is the least robust" ([Acta Informatica 7(2), 1976](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00265771)). The follow-on literature states flatly that "these criteria are not based on mathematical models and cannot be proved to be optimal" ([UBC TR-80-05](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/tr/1980/TR-80-05.pdf)). *Which* process bears the cost, and for how long, was never posed as a research question.

The dangerous prior art is the live one. **thrash-protect detects thrashing from `/proc/vmstat` swap counters and sends SIGSTOP/SIGCONT; its third victim-selection algorithm is page-fault counts; and it already rotates** — frozen PIDs are handled by "get from the head of the list sometimes, pop from the tail most of the times," precisely because a pure stack "may permanently freeze relatively innocent processes" while a pure queue "is inefficient and causes paging" ([thrash-protect README](https://github.com/tobixen/thrash-protect)). A claim that freeze-plus-rotation is new will be refuted by one README. What that same README concedes is the real opening, and it should be quoted in the thesis rather than paraphrased. It states outright that the tool "is not optimized to be 'fair'", with process B flapping while A runs at 100%. Its fault-count victim algorithm "produces false positives and misses write-only memory growth." Black- and whitelisting weight the suspension score rather than acting as hard rules, so a whitelisted terminal can still become unusable under pressure because whitelisting does not keep its code resident — the README itself suggests `memory.min`/`MemoryMin=` as a fix it does not implement. It does not read PSI; `/proc/pressure/memory` "will probably be used" in a future version. And its thresholds misfire on modern storage: disk swap pages are weighted 128× (HDD) or 8× (SSD) against zswap pages, the author "realized the default values aren't optimized for SSDs," and a zram laptop "froze processes 37 + 4 times in eleven hours without real pressure" ([PR #42](https://github.com/tobixen/thrash-protect/pull/42)) — a measured limit-cycle in the field, in the closest baseline, which is exactly the chatter a control-theoretic treatment of STASIS would predict.

Three further structural differences separate STASIS from both Denning and thrash-protect, and each is independently citable. The signal is categorically different: PSI "measures lost work from resource shortage" rather than counting events, and Meta's own framing contrasts it explicitly with "proxy metrics such as page-fault rates" ([TMO, ASPLOS'22](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dskarlat/publications/tmo_asplos22.pdf)). The actuator is categorically different: `cgroup.freeze` (Linux 5.2) stops a whole subtree by extending jobctl-stop, so frozen tasks remain killable and ptraceable, the freeze completes asynchronously with a `cgroup.events:frozen` notification, and `cgroup.stat.local:frozen_usec` reports cumulative frozen time ([kernel.org cgroup-v2](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/latest/admin-guide/cgroup-v2.html)) — which simultaneously fixes thrash-protect's documented SIGSTOP job-control hazards and *supplies the fairness currency a rotation policy needs*. And attribution is available where PSI deliberately withholds it: the `memory.stat` `workingset_refault_anon`/`workingset_refault_file` counters are per-cgroup refaults of recently evicted pages, i.e. the thrash itself, attributable to a tenant ([kernel.org cgroup-v2](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/latest/admin-guide/cgroup-v2.html)). Across the whole modern stack the three actions are implemented by disjoint tools and **none of them implements fairness or admission control**: systemd-oomd, earlyoom and nohang kill; thrash-protect and rtux pause; `memory.high`, `memory.reclaim`, DAMOS and Senpai reclaim. The closest things to fairness anywhere are static priority and exclusion lists — `ManagedOOMPreference=avoid|omit` ([systemd.resource-control(5)](https://www.freedesktop.org/software/systemd/man/latest/systemd.resource-control.html)), earlyoom's `--prefer/--avoid/--ignore` ([earlyoom README](https://github.com/rfjakob/earlyoom)) — plus AIX's `v_exempt_secs` reactivation exemption, which appears in [vendor documentation](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/ssw_aix_71/performance/mem_load_control_alg.html) with no evaluation, no fairness metric and no comparison against alternatives.

## The seam is plumbed and unoccupied: what the scx code search found

The sched_ext half of the claim now rests on code, not on search results. A shallow clone of `sched-ext/scx` at commit `0fb3264` (October 2026) was searched across 722 `.c`/`.bpf.c`/`.h`/`.rs` files, excluding the generated BTF dump in `scheds/vmlinux/` where all naive grep hits land. `grep -E 'maj_flt|min_flt'` returned **zero hits**: no scheduler in the upstream tree reads per-task fault counters. Every surviving `page.?fault` hit is non-competing — comments explaining why `scx_rustland_core` calls `mlockall()` to *avoid* faulting (a kthread needed to resolve a fault would deadlock against the scheduler waiting on it), string parsing of the perf event name `"page-faults"` for a generic `--perf-event` CLI flag, and `scx_rustland` counting *its own process's* faults as a self-health metric.

The load-bearing finding is the fourth one. `scheds/include/scx/percpu.bpf.h` already declares, as weak ksyms with ready-made per-CPU accessors, `struct psi_group_cpu psi_group_cpu` and `struct vm_event_state vm_event_states` — the latter being the per-CPU vmstat array carrying `pgmajfault`, `pswpin` and `pswpout` — together with `DEFINE_PER_CPU_PTR_FUNC` and `DEFINE_THIS_CPU_PTR_FUNC` wrappers. **A call-site search for all four symbols across the whole tree returns only the declarations and the macro definitions.** Six schedulers include that header — `scx_rustland_core`, `scx_beerland`, `scx_bpfland`, `scx_cosmos`, `scx_eevdf`, `scx_p2dq` — and all six use it for LLC domains, cpufreq/`sugov` and cpustat, while none touches the PSI or vmstat accessors sitting in the same file. Meanwhile scx's own TUI builds a swap summary panel (`swap_total_kb`, `swap_free_kb`, `delta_swap_in`) in `tools/scxtop/src/columns.rs`, so the ecosystem **displays** swap activity to a human while no scheduler consumes it as input. The seam is not merely unexplored; it is plumbed and unoccupied. The limits of that check matter and should be stated in the thesis: it covers the upstream tree only, not out-of-tree or vendor schedulers or research code; it is lexical, so a read through an unexpected indirection would not match; and the shallow clone made it impossible to date when the PSI/vmstat ksyms were added.

This is consistent with everything else the notes establish about sched_ext. The documented `struct sched_ext_ops` interface exposes no memory callback, no fault hook, no reclaim hook and no PSI accessor ([kernel.org sched-ext](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/latest/scheduler/sched-ext.html)). `scx_lavd`, the flagship interactivity scheduler, names latency-criticality and CPU topology as its inputs and does not mention memory ([scx_lavd README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sched-ext/scx/main/scheds/rust/scx_lavd/README.md)). The published 2026 roadmap lists GPU awareness and energy-aware abstractions; memory awareness is absent ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/sched-ext-future-plans-2026)). The important distinction to draw pre-emptively is that scx is thoroughly *memory-hierarchy-locality*-aware (where a task's cache lines are) and nowhere *memory-capacity*- or *paging*-aware (whether its working set fits). Reading `p->maj_flt` should be verifier-legal, since `p` arrives as a trusted `PTR_TO_BTF_ID` struct_ops callback argument and `maj_flt` is a direct scalar member, whereas walking `p->mm->...` is a pointer walk and materially riskier ([kfuncs documentation](https://docs.kernel.org/bpf/kfuncs.html); [PTR_TRUSTED series](https://lkml.rescloud.iu.edu/2211.2/01944.html)) — but **no source confirms that specific read inside a sched_ext program, so it remains an empirical claim to be demonstrated on the target kernel, not a cited one.**

### The wording to use, verbatim

The following claims are supported by the notes and should be used as written.

> As of the October 2026 upstream tree (commit `0fb3264`), no sched_ext scheduler reads per-task page-fault counters, and the per-CPU PSI and vmstat accessors provided by `scx/percpu.bpf.h` have no callers.

> We are not aware of any sched_ext scheduler that measures per-task page-fault frequency inside the BPF scheduler and uses it as a scheduling input.

> The control signal lives in the CPU scheduler, the actuator lives in the memory cgroup, and the loop is closed across the two subsystems. Every individual component has prior art; this cross-subsystem coupling does not.

> No shipped or upstream system combines memory-pressure detection with bounded per-victim freeze time, fair rotation, or admission control. thrash-protect disclaims fairness; rtux lists admission control as future work; systemd-oomd, earlyoom and nohang kill; DAMOS and Senpai reclaim.

### The claims that will be refuted

Three tempting claims are false and must not appear. **"No CPU scheduler has ever reacted to paging"** is refuted by classical load control, PFF, the Linux swap token, and above all by **US Patent 9,465,656, "Scheduler penalty for swapping activity"** — a scheduler that penalises tasks for swapping activity, which is the most direct prior art for the core idea and must be cited and distinguished ([USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9465656)). **"No sched_ext scheduler is memory-aware"** is refuted by **CXLAimPod** (arXiv 2508.15980), an eBPF scheduler on sched_ext using cgroup hints and per-task PMU counters for CXL placement ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15980v1)); the honest distinction is clean — CXLAimPod's signal is memory-bandwidth and duplex characteristics, its action is placement within a NUMA node, it reads no fault counters and it throttles nothing. And **"nobody has coupled paging to per-process scheduling treatment"** is refuted by the **Linux swap token**, which granted a token to one faulting process at a time so it could claim extra memory, finish, and release pages, with a later "preempt" variant tracking swapped-out pages per process ([Thrashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrashing_(computer_science))). Note also the one quote most threatening to the project's framing, which is better confronted than ignored: at LSF/MM/BPF 2026, BPF maintainer Alexei Starovoitov said **"sched_ext was a mistake"** because no production schedulers came with it ([LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/1072538/)).

## Ranked features: baselines, then control theory, then real workloads

The ranking is novelty impact divided by effort for one student on one 4 GB VM, with the pre-registration impact treated as a hard constraint rather than a footnote.

| # | Feature | Claim it supports | Precedent | Effort | Pre-registration impact |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Named-baseline comparison: thrash-protect, earlyoom, systemd-oomd, do-nothing | "Measured against the state of the practice, not against a textbook" | thrash-protect's own documented pathologies; no critique of it exists | **Low** (days) | **Untouched** — same thresholds, extra treatment arms |
| 2 | Formal control-theoretic treatment of the existing control law | "Load control as a feedback controller, not a heuristic" | SASO properties; Diao et al.; no formal treatment of thrashing control found | **Medium**, analysis-heavy, almost no code | **Untouched** *if* the law is analysed as implemented; **invalidating** if the law changes |
| 3 | Real-workload replacement for synthetic jobs | "It works on something real" | Hermes (RocksDB/Redis under pressure); Caladan's memcached-vs-batch shape | Medium | **New pre-registered arm** — new workloads mean new hypotheses; do not touch arm 1 |
| 4 | Fairness metric on existing runs (Jain's index over `frozen_usec`) | "Fairness is measured, not asserted" | Jain's index in cache fairness (0.7–0.85 reported); DRF lineage; Autopilot's OOM-tolerance classes | Low–Medium | **Untouched** as a new metric on existing traces; **new arm** if the rotation quantum changes |
| 5 | Derivative/lead term (PSI or fault-rate slope) as a D-term | "Acts before onset" at a tenth of ML's cost | TMO's subliminal-pressure counter-argument; xTier's dead-band hysteresis | Low–Medium | **New arm** — the trigger changes |
| 6 | DAMON working-set estimation instead of `memory.peak` | "The admission predicate's estimator is a measured design choice" | DAMON/DAMOS WSS estimation; eBPF WSS estimation | Medium | **New arm** — the admission threshold is defined in terms of the estimator |
| 7 | Energy as an explicit proxy (swap bytes, controller CPU-seconds) | "The controller's own cost is accounted" | None found linking thrashing control to energy; RAPL unreliable in a VM | Low–Medium | **Untouched** — observation only |
| 8 | Predictive (ML) thrashing trigger | "Forecast-driven preemption" | eBPF+LightGBM for *fragmentation*, not pressure; Maas et al. on allocation lifetimes | **High** | **Invalidating** — a predictive trigger replaces the PSI trigger |
| 9 | CRIU checkpoint/restore instead of freeze | "Checkpoint beats freeze" — probably false | HFSP: suspend/resume swap traffic at disk raw speed; CRIU dominated by the memory image path | High | **Invalidating** — changes the actuator the hypotheses are about |
| 10 | Container/Kubernetes integration; CXL/tiered memory | Near-zero novelty here; cannot be done honestly on one VM | K8s burstable QoS; TPP, HybridTier, xTier, TierBPF | Medium–Very High | Varies; not worth the cost |

**Feature 1 is the highest value per unit of effort in the entire project and it costs the pre-registration nothing.** thrash-protect is installable, documents its own three victim-selection algorithms, and documents its own failure modes, which means the baseline hands you your hypotheses ([README](https://github.com/tobixen/thrash-protect)). earlyoom adds a second measurable axis in controller overhead, via its open CPU-usage-versus-check-interval tradeoff ([issue #61](https://github.com/rfjakob/earlyoom/issues/61)). systemd-oomd is a zero-install baseline already on Ubuntu, configured per unit through `ManagedOOMMemoryPressure=` with a default pressure limit of 60% ([systemd-oomd(8)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man8/systemd-oomd.service.8.html)) — and it is a baseline with a documented reputation for over-aggression, Ubuntu's own maintainers reporting that "there have been reports of systemd-oomd killing user applications too frequently (e.g. browsers, IDEs, and gnome-shell in some cases)" ([ubuntu-devel](https://lists.ubuntu.com/archives/ubuntu-devel/2022-June/042116.html)). One caution carried from the notes: sources disagree on whether the relevant key is `DefaultMemoryPressureDurationSec` or a `...USec` variant, and the per-unit `ManagedOOMMemoryPressureDurationSec=` only arrived in systemd v257, so verify against `systemd-oomd(8)` and `systemd.resource-control(5)` before writing either into a config. Because this feature converts "Denning re-implemented" into "measured against the state of the practice," and because no reproduction study of these tools exists in the literature, the resulting numbers are genuinely new data.

**Feature 2 is the only item that could plausibly be called a conceptual contribution, and it is nearly free in code.** No formal stability or convergence analysis of a thrashing controller was found — no Lyapunov argument, no AIMD analysis, no transfer-function model of a memory load-control loop — while the machinery for doing exactly this to computing systems is established and citable through the SASO properties (stability, accuracy, settling time, overshoot) and the discipline of designing loops to "avoid wild oscillations, hit target response times, and settle quickly" ([Abdelzaher et al.](https://www.eecs.umich.edu/courses/eecs571/reading/control-to-computer-zaher.pdf)). Two framings make the claim bounded and defensible. First, computing systems "behave acceptably only in certain combinations of workload and configuration, so stability is usually assessed within an **operating region** rather than globally" — which licenses a claim of stability over the pre-registered operating region rather than universally. Second, the three named open challenges in control of self-managing systems — "building usable resource models, handling sensor delays, and accounting for lead times in actuator actions" ([Diao et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/3236201_A_control_theory_foundation_for_self-managing_computing_systems)) — are all three present in STASIS as PSI window lag, reclaim latency, and freeze/thaw lead time. Be honest about the likely outcome: STASIS's law as written (halve the active set under pressure, admit when the estimate fits) is MIMD-like, a switched system with a saturating actuator, and a rigorous treatment will more probably *prove it can oscillate* than prove it stable. **That negative result is the stronger chapter.** The tractable scope is to model the loop as a discrete-time switched system with state equal to active-set size and a one-sample measurement delay, derive the dead-band condition under which no limit cycle exists, show whether the current parameterisation satisfies it, predict the limit-cycle period when it does not, and then test that prediction against the already-collected replay traces. Prediction-then-verification against existing data is a real contribution that requires no re-running of frozen experiments. Reframing the law as MIMD and asking the AIMD question explicitly is reviewer-legible and, as far as the notes could establish, unclaimed for thrashing — but that is a failure to find, not a proof, and the AIMD variant should be *proposed* as further work rather than implemented, because changing the law to make it provable invalidates the pre-registration.

**Feature 3 is what a viva will ask for first and it is the expensive one.** No standard memory-pressure suite exists; the de facto practice is to assemble one. Hermes is the closest template, evaluating RocksDB and Redis query latency and SLO violations "under different memory pressure levels" and reporting up to 84.3% fewer SLO violations against glibc/jemalloc/TCMalloc ([arXiv 2109.02922](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.02922v1)). Caladan supplies the canonical experimental shape — memcached as the latency-critical tenant against a best-effort batch job, with tail latency as the headline number ([OSDI'20](https://pdos.csail.mit.edu/papers/caladan:osdi20.pdf)). The concrete recommendation is memcached driven by `memtier_benchmark`, or RocksDB, as the protected interactive tenant, with kernel compile and/or RocksDB bulk-load as the memory-hungry parked jobs, keeping cyclictest as a cheap sanity probe. Two warnings: kernel compile is defensible as a self-evidently real memory-hungry batch job but is **not** a cited standard benchmark, and an LLM inference server on a 4 GB VM is not credible and should not be attempted.

**Feature 4 exploits the thinnest-covered area in the whole survey.** No peer-reviewed study was found combining memory reclaim, multi-tenant fairness, and a fairness metric such as Jain's index; the existing uses of Jain's index in this space are in cache allocation, reporting values around 0.7–0.85 ([arXiv 2201.09833](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09833)), while memory fairness is approached as *utility* rather than *equality of share* — MemExchange matching tenants by marginal utility ([arXiv 2607.11579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.11579)), Equilibria adding per-container fair-share controls to CXL tiering ([arXiv 2602.08800](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08800v1)). Because `cgroup.stat.local:frozen_usec` already records cumulative frozen time per victim, computing Jain's index over frozen time across tenants is a handful of lines over traces that already exist, and it is the direct quantitative answer to thrash-protect's self-reported unfairness. Autopilot's OOM-tolerance classes supply a ready-made priority taxonomy with a real citation if priority classes are wanted ([EuroSys'20](https://john.e-wilkes.com/papers/2020-EuroSys-Autopilot.pdf)).

**Features 5 through 10 are where a solo student loses the dissertation.** The predictive trigger is attractive and poorly supported: the nearest work uses eBPF telemetry plus LightGBM to predict memory *fragmentation* and defragment pre-emptively — right method, wrong target variable ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3703935.3704087)) — and TMO's design philosophy is a standing counter-argument, since Senpai "needs no offline profiling" and acts continuously at a subliminal pressure level, so a reviewer can fairly ask why prediction beats acting earlier on a continuous signal. The cheap substitute is feature 5's D-term, which supports the same "acts before onset" claim at a fraction of the work and folds into the control-theory chapter. CRIU is worse than it looks: HFSP measured suspend/resume swap traffic running "roughly at disk raw-speed" ([arXiv 1302.2749](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.2749)), VAS-CRIU identifies filesystem write/read of memory-page image files as CRIU's dominant cost ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10192799)), and one industrial report notes that for larger models "restore time actually exceeds cold-start time, which negates the benefit of checkpointing" ([Photoroom](https://www.photoroom.com/inside-photoroom/how-we-cut-gpu-cold-starts-from-minutes-to-seconds-with-memory-checkpointing)). That makes CRIU an excellent *discussion* section — "why freezing in place beats checkpointing under thrashing, with numbers" — and a poor implementation target. Tiered memory cannot be evaluated honestly on one VM against TPP, HybridTier, xTier and TierBPF, though a zram/zswap tier is a legitimate cheap stand-in, and thrash-protect's zram findings give a real citable phenomenon to study on one machine.

The governing rule across all of these: **the pre-registration is the project's most unusual asset relative to systems-venue norms, so protect it.** Treat every new feature as a new arm with its own pre-registered hypothesis appended with a date, never as an edit to arm 1. Reporting "H1–H4 pre-registered on date X and unchanged; H5–H7 pre-registered later for the real-workload arm" is far more credible under examination than a single frozen set that quietly grew.

## rtux/pressured is concurrent independent work and must be re-checked before submission

**rtux** (daemon name `pressured`) is a Rust PSI and cgroup-v2 desktop daemon, created **July 2026**, that describes itself as "a legible, reversible memory-pressure daemon for Linux desktops that freezes runaway apps instead of killing them, and tells you what it did" ([github.com/justinstimatze/rtux](https://github.com/justinstimatze/rtux)). Its escalation ladder — Pin, Cap, Pause, Close — is STASIS's actuator set in miniature, and at the time of the notes it had 0 stars, 48 commits, no releases and 0 forks, with one reported field observation of pausing a 1.1 GB browser while keeping the desktop responsive. Two facts make it concurrent rather than pre-emptive: it has a **static priority** notion (protecting the cursor and shell first) but **no rotation**, and its author lists "refusing a load that won't fit before it arrives" — admission control — explicitly as "the work ahead." Its README does not even name the kernel mechanism it uses, saying only that an app is "freezing it whole and intact" without stating whether that is `cgroup.freeze` or SIGSTOP, and its `DESIGN.md` was not retrievable, so whether any rotation exists in code is unverified. The correct posture is therefore to phrase the novelty claim as concurrent and independent rather than as absent prior art, to cite rtux as such, and to **re-check the repository near submission** — a release, a rotation commit, or an admission-control feature landing between now and the viva would change what can be claimed.

Three other concurrency threats deserve a watch list. The kernel community is actively building the *other* half of the bridge: LSF/MM/BPF 2026 carried sessions on BPF OOM handlers (Roman Gushchin, struct_ops based), BPF memcg callbacks such as `bpf_memcg_charge_succeed()` to kick off background reclaim, BPF NUMA balancing, and `reclaim_ext`/`memcg_ext` proposals, with Gushchin's open question being "what happens if a faulty program leaves the system unable to reclaim memory" ([LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/1072538/)); Hui Zhu's BPF proactive-memcg-reclaim series was at v10 as of September 2026 ([LKML](https://ratatoskr.run/lkml/2026/09/17549861/t)). This is simultaneously the best and worst news — it proves the direction is live and timely, and it means the kernel will shortly make the coupling easy. Write the thesis as "what a scheduler-coupled policy can do *before* BPF-MM hooks land, and what it tells us about which hooks are needed," a contribution the LWN piece's own open questions invite. A 2026 RFC to add an in-kernel PSI auto-monitor that captures contributing tasks at the moment thresholds are breached would narrow the attribution gap STASIS exploits, and its merge status is unconfirmed ([RFC](https://lkml.iu.edu/2607.2/07955.html)). And MGLRU — one of the estimator options — has stalled: LWN reported in March 2026 that progress has stopped, it "still is not enabled on many systems," and "another has called for it to be removed entirely" ([LWN 1060967](https://lwn.net/Articles/1060967/)), so any design depending on `/sys/kernel/debug/lru_gen` should be able to fall back to `memory.stat` refault counters.

## What the notes could not establish, and where the report is thinner than it reads

Several load-bearing findings are failures-to-find rather than proofs of absence, and the thesis should say so in the same breath as the claim. **No paper matching STASIS's full combination was found, but no survey of post-PSI thrashing-control systems was found either** — so the search space itself is unmapped, and absence of a found paper is weak evidence of absence. **No formal stability treatment of a thrashing controller was found**, but the older multiprogramming-level-control literature (Multics load control, the 1970s "level of multiprogramming" controllers) could not be covered, and that is precisely where such an analysis would most plausibly be buried; a targeted citation search on Denning's 1968 "Thrashing: Its Causes and Prevention" should be run before the claim is made in writing. **No classical paper (1968–1990) studying fairness, starvation or rotation among deactivated processes was found**, but that negative rests on web-reachable sources and should be backed by ACM DL and IEEE Xplore full-text searches before being asserted as "never." **No source makes the AIMD-to-thrashing mapping**, which means it could not be confirmed as unclaimed. **No published critique or reproduction study of thrash-protect, earlyoom or nohang exists** — the evidence of their failure modes is the maintainers' own READMEs and issues, credible but self-reported, which is exactly why STASIS measuring it is new.

Specific source-quality caveats carry forward. Meta's 20–32% memory saving and Senpai's behaviour come from Meta's own engineering blog, with the ASPLOS'22/CACM versions as the peer-reviewed anchors; DAMON's overhead numbers (≲1% of one CPU, independent of memory size) are self-reported by the developer on specific setups and not independently reproduced; Google's ~47% app-launch improvement from MGLRU is a vendor figure. The 2026-dated arXiv entries — xTier, Equilibria, MemExchange, TierBPF, CrystalMem, DA-DRF — were seen only through search results, so their venues and peer-review status are **unverified** and they must be cited as preprints; feature 4's and feature 6's precedent claims in particular rest on secondary listings. CXLAimPod's peer-review status is likewise unverified, as is whether its code is public. Much of the classical material in the notes arrives through tertiary educational sites and Wikipedia and is **not citable in a dissertation**: the primary works must be obtained directly, and two scanned primary PDFs (Denning's AFIPS 1968 paper and the Chu & Opderbeck PFF scan) are archived locally but have no text layer, so installing poppler and running OCR would close the largest remaining gaps — the exact 1968 load-control wording and PFF's measured results versus the working set, neither of which could be verified. The exact venue and year of the original PFF paper remain unresolved between 1972, 1973 and 1976; only the decade is reliable. Carr & Hennessy's WSCLOCK (SOSP 1981) could not be fetched and is the single most important source to obtain, since it concedes the working-set policy's overhead critique while accepting its load-control goal. Finally, two claims about STASIS's own mechanism are empirical rather than cited: that `p->maj_flt` is readable inside a sched_ext struct_ops program, and that "major faults per millisecond of the process's own CPU time" is not a prior named metric — on the latter, treat it as **probably not novel in kind, possibly novel in form**, because a reviewer may reasonably call it a re-parameterisation of PFF, which is faults per unit of virtual time between faults.

## Conclusion

The shape of the opportunity is the inverse of what it looks like from inside the project. STASIS's architecture is almost entirely prior art, and its strongest asset is not the design but the **evidence base nobody has built**: there is no survey of post-PSI thrashing control, no reproduction study of the kill-based and freeze-based daemons people actually run, no accuracy figure for any working-set estimator against ground truth, no fairness metric ever applied to memory reclaim, and no stability analysis of any thrashing controller. A master's thesis is permitted to be an evaluation contribution, and on one 4 GB VM an evaluation contribution is the only kind that can be made honestly. The two features that cost the least — installing three named baselines and analysing the existing control law as a switched system — are also the two that leave the frozen pre-registration completely untouched, which is an unusually clean alignment of scientific value with practical constraint.

One strategic reframing follows from the code search and should shape the writing. The finding that scx already ships per-CPU PSI and vmstat accessors with no callers converts the novelty claim from a literature argument into a structural observation a reader can verify in a minute, and it explains *why* the gap exists: sched_ext exposes no first-class memory input, and the BPF-for-MM effort keeps every decision inside MM, so nobody has had a reason to bridge the two. That also dates the contribution. The bridge is being built from the memory side right now, which means the durable claim is not "we coupled the scheduler to memory" but "we measured what a scheduler-coupled policy buys, before the hooks existed, and showed which hooks are worth having." Written that way, the thesis survives the arrival of BPF-MM instead of being obsoleted by it.
